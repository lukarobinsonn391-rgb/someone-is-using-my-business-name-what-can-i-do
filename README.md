<h1><strong>Someone Is Using My Business Name. What Can I Do in 2026?</strong></h1>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If someone is using your business name, don't fire off an angry message yet. Start by working out what rights you actually have, document what the other business is doing, and decide whether it's truly confusing customers.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In many cases you can resolve it with a polite note or a formal cease-and-desist letter, and if not, you can escalate through platform complaints, a trademark attorney, or the courts.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The strength of your position depends on whether you hold a registered trademark, how long you've used the name, where you operate, and whether the two businesses overlap.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This guide walks through the steps in a sensible order. It's general information for US-style trademark law, not legal advice, and rules differ by country and state.</span></p>
<h2><strong>1. Know What Protection You Actually Have</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many owners assume registering a company with the state protects their name everywhere. It doesn't.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">There are several layers, and each covers something different:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Business entity or trade name registration.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Registering an LLC, corporation, or "doing business as" (DBA) name with your state usually stops another entity from registering the identical name in that state.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">It doesn't give you rights against businesses in other states or in other industries, and it doesn't override a federal trademark someone else holds.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Common law trademark rights.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> These arise automatically when you use a name in commerce to identify your goods or services.&nbsp;</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">They generally cover only the area where you operate and are known, and they're harder to prove than a registration.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Federal trademark registration.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> A registration with the US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) gives broader, nationwide protection and makes enforcement easier.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sole proprietors using only a DBA have the thinnest protection of all. If your name matters to the business, how you set up the business matters too.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This guide to</span><a href="https://de-epea.readme.io/reference/how-to-choose-and-register-your-business-structure-complete-guide"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">matching structure to situation</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> explains how different structures and registrations work, so you know what your name filing actually covers.</span></p>
<p><strong>Try this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Write down your registration status in three columns: state filing, federal trademark, and date you first used the name publicly. That tells you where you stand.</span></p>
<h2><strong>2. Document Everything and Keep Your Records Clean</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Before you contact anyone, build your evidence. Strong records show when you started using your name and how the other party is using theirs.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Collect:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Screenshots and links</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> of the other business's website, social accounts, ads, listings, and invoices, with dates.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Proof of your first use:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> old invoices, website archives, business cards, advertising, and press coverage.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Examples of confusion:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> misdirected emails, calls, reviews, or messages from customers who thought the two businesses were the same.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Your registrations:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> state filings, domain registrations, and any trademark filings.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Evidence of your reach:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> where your customers are, and how much you've spent on marketing.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tidy financial records help too. Clear, separate business accounts make it easier to show that your name was used in real commerce, with real sales, since a certain date.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This is one reason to</span><a href="https://lukarobinsonn391.systeme.io/three-steps-business-owners-can-take-to-protect-th"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">keep business and personal money strictly separate</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, which also protects you if disputes become formal.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Save everything in one folder, and don't edit or delete anything, since you may need it later.</span></p>
<p><strong>Try this:</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Capture dated screenshots of every page where the other business uses your name, including its URL.</span></p>
<h2><strong>3. Decide Whether It's Really Infringement</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not every similar name is illegal. Trademark law centers on the likelihood of confusion: whether consumers are likely to mistake one business for the other or assume they're connected.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Courts and the USPTO look at how similar the names are, how related the goods or services are, where each business operates, and whether real confusion has occurred.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The USPTO explains that trademark confusion can arise even when two names aren't identical.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Similarities in sound, appearance, meaning, and the relationship between the products or services can all influence the decision.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Its official</span><a href="https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">likelihood of confusion guidance</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> explains what to consider before deciding whether another business may be infringing on your trademark.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A few examples of how this plays out:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Same name, same industry, same area. Strong case for confusion.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Similar name, related industries. Possible case, depending on the facts.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Same name, unrelated industries or distant markets. Often allowed, especially if neither name is famous.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Name used only as a corporate name, not to sell goods or services. This may not count as trademark use at all.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Timing matters as well. Priority usually goes to the party who used the name first in a given area, or who registered first at the federal level.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If the other business started using the name before you did, you may be the one at risk.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Before you act, search for other users of the name, including federal and state trademark records, state business registries, domain names, social platforms, and general web results.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The USPTO retired its older TESS search tool in 2023, and its current trademark search system replaced it.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Searching widely helps you see whether the other party has stronger rights than you assumed.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Try this: Ask yourself: could a customer reasonably think we're the same company? If yes, your case is stronger.</span></p>
<h2><strong>4. Consider a Friendly First Contact</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If the overlap looks accidental, a short, calm message can solve the problem fast. Many small businesses don't know a name. Keep it factual:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">State who you are and how long you've used the name.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Explain that you've noticed customers confusing the two businesses, if that's true.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ask them to change or distinguish their name, or to discuss options.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Avoid threats, accusations, or promises about outcomes.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Be careful not to claim rights you don't have. If your position is shaky, a heavy-handed message can backfire and invite a counterclaim.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you're unsure, speak with an attorney before sending anything.</span></p>
<h2><strong>5. Send a Cease-and-Desist Letter If Needed</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If the friendly approach fails or the use looks deliberate, a cease-and-desist letter is the usual next step.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It's a written demand that the other business stop using the name, and it usually:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Identifies your rights, such as a trademark registration or your first-use date</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Describes the problem and the confusion it causes</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Demands specific actions, such as stopping use, changing domains, or removing listings</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sets a reasonable deadline</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Reserves your right to take further action</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A cease-and-desist letter isn't a court order, and it doesn't force anyone to comply on its own.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But it creates a record that you objected, which can matter later, and many disputes end at this stage.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A letter from a trademark attorney carries more weight and helps avoid mistakes, such as overstating your claims.</span></p>
<h2><strong>6. Use Platform and Domain Complaint Tools</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Online platforms often have faster routes than courts, especially when the infringement is on their services:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Social media platforms</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> typically have trademark or impersonation reporting forms. You'll usually need to show your trademark rights, and some require a registration.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Marketplaces and app stores</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> have brand-protection or trademark complaint processes.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Search ads.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Search engines have policies on trademark use in ads. Check their current rules before you complain.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Domain names.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> If someone registered a domain that copies your name in bad faith, you may be able to file a complaint under the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP), usually administered by WIPO and other providers, or pursue claims under US cybersquatting law. You generally need trademark rights to succeed.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Web hosts and registrars</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> may take down clearly infringing or fraudulent sites if you report them with evidence.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Policies vary and change, so read each platform's current instructions.</span></p>
<h2><strong>7. Escalate With Legal Help</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If the other business ignores you or the problem is serious, talk to a trademark attorney. They can assess your priority, the strength of your claim, and the likely cost.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Options may include:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Negotiating a settlement or coexistence agreement,</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> such as limits on territory, spelling, or logos.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Opposing or challenging their trademark application</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> at the USPTO if they file for the name.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Filing a lawsuit</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> for infringement or unfair competition, which can seek court orders to stop the use and, in some cases, money damages.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Seeking an injunction</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in urgent cases where confusion or harm is severe.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Legal action can be expensive and slow, so weigh the costs against the value of your name.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many owners find coexistence or a negotiated change is more practical than a courtroom fight.</span></p>
<h2><strong>8. Strengthen Your Own Rights Going Forward</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Whether or not you resolve this dispute, close the gaps that made you vulnerable:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Consider federal trademark registration</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> if the name is central to your business. It offers nationwide protection and clearer evidence of ownership.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Register your domain names,</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> including common variations and misspellings.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Claim your social media handles</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> on the platforms your customers use.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Use the name consistently</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and add the correct trademark notice, such as &trade;, once you claim rights.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Set up monitoring.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Search alerts and periodic checks for new look-alikes can catch problems early.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Keep records of use</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> with dates, sales, and advertising.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you haven't yet formed or filed your business properly, make sure your entity name and trade names are registered where you operate.</span></p>
<h2><strong>9. Protect Your Customers and Reputation</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">While you sort things out, communicate clearly with your customers:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Make your branding distinctive.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Use your logo, colors, and a clear tagline to stand apart.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Post a short notice</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> on your site if customers are being misled, such as "We are not affiliated with [other business]."</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Respond to confusion promptly,</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and point people to your real contact details.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Watch for scams.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> If someone is using your name to collect payments or impersonate you, report it to the platform, your bank or payment processor, and local authorities, and warn customers.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Keep your tone professional</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> in public. Venting online can create legal risk.</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>10. When to Consider Changing Your Name</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sometimes the practical answer is to rebrand. Consider it if:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The other business has stronger or earlier rights.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A court fight would cost more than the name is worth.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Confusion keeps hurting your business and the other side won't budge.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">You're early in the brand's life, with little to lose.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you rebrand, search carefully first, register the new name properly, secure the domain and handles, and tell your customers about the change.</span></p>

<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Acting without evidence.</strong></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Waiting too long.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Delay can weaken your claim.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Ignoring a letter</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> if the other side contacts you. Take it seriously and get advice.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Copying the other side's tactics.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Stay factual and professional.</span></li>

</ul>

<h2><strong>The Bottom Line</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Finding someone using your business name is stressful, but you have a clear path: know your rights, document the problem, judge whether it's really confusing, and escalate step by step, from a friendly note to a cease-and-desist letter.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Platform complaints, and legal help if needed. Then protect yourself going forward with proper registrations and monitoring.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The earlier you act and the better your records, the stronger your position.</span></p>
